What this is

A to-do list that assumes you cannot fully trust a plan your earlier self made.

Strange Request is an experimental to-do list. It starts from John Perry's essay on structured procrastination, which suggests that people who put things off can still get a great deal done. The trick is to keep one important task at the top of the list and do everything else as a way of avoiding it. This version keeps that joke and builds its mechanics on findings from procrastination research.

Notes from a stranger

Every note on the list was written earlier, in some mood, with a guess about how today would go. Research on the future self, associated with Hal Hershfield and drawing on the philosopher Derek Parfit, finds that people tend to think about their future self in ways that resemble thinking about another person. A weaker sense of connection to that person goes with a preference for smaller, sooner rewards.

If that is roughly right, a plan is a promise made on someone else's behalf. The list keeps that in view. Each note shows who wrote it, how they felt and how urgent they thought it was. You review each note before acting on it, the way you would check a list you found.

How each part works

Mood and urgency on each note
You record how you felt and how urgent the note seemed when you wrote it. Plans tend to be made in a calm, abstract state and carried out in a tired or tempted one. George Loewenstein called this the hot-cold empathy gap. With the mood shown beside the note, today's you can discount it.
Deadlines and the score line
People underestimate how long tasks take, even when similar tasks ran late before. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named this the planning fallacy. When you finish a note that had a deadline, the line under Case notes compares the deadline with the day you finished, so you build a record of how reliable those deadlines are.
Still open and red herring
This is Perry's trick made visible. Your most urgent note stays at the top, dimmed. The next one is promoted as the red herring, the thing to do first. Piers Steel's temporal motivation theory says we put off tasks whose payoff is distant or whose effort is unpleasant, and drift to whatever is nearer and easier. The desk uses that drift.
Keep, Push down, Amend
Keep says the plan still holds. Push down admits it is not happening today and does not delete it. Amend rewrites the note and leaves the old wording struck through, so the change stays visible. George Ainslie's work on hyperbolic discounting suggests our preferences shift as the moment of action gets close, so your earlier and later selves can disagree sincerely. These controls let you renegotiate with them.
Case notes
Anything you finish that is not the top note is logged as work done instead of the top job. You can also add entries by hand. Perry argues that this kind of avoidance can be productive. The log lets you check whether that holds for you, and whether the notes you keep pushing down ever mattered.
Anonymous tip
An optional switch that asks Claude to reword every note as if a different person had written it. Your own wording stays underneath. This one is a hunch more than a research finding. Seeing a note in unfamiliar words may loosen your attachment to the first version, and it makes the list read like something you found. The switch only appears where the page can reach Claude. If you do not see it, it is not available in this copy.

What the research says

Most researchers treat procrastination as a failure of self-regulation and not as a time-management problem. Steel's 2007 meta-analysis found the strongest links to task aversiveness, low confidence in one's ability to do the task, impulsiveness and low conscientiousness. Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl describe procrastination as putting short-term mood repair ahead of the long-term interests of the future self.

This tool leans on that mainstream work. It makes no claims about trauma or clinical conditions.

What it does not claim

Nobody has tested whether this arrangement reduces procrastination. The link between each feature and its source is design reasoning, and the research behind each idea describes general tendencies, not what will work for you. This is a toy and an experiment. It is not therapy and not a clinical tool. If procrastination is causing you real difficulty, a professional can help in ways a to-do list cannot.

Your data

Notes, case notes and your edits are saved in this browser, on this device, and are not uploaded anywhere. Clearing your browser data erases them. If you switch on Anonymous tip, the text of your notes is sent to Claude to be reworded. That uses your own Claude usage, and the page asks your permission first.

The four example notes are samples. Clear examples removes them.
